Inside Nursing Home Fire Safety — Finding Patterns in Citations

This post summarizes notebook results from the CMS nursing-home fire-safety citations dataset. It examines where citations cluster, which deficiencies appear most often, and whether a simple model can help flag high-severity risk.

Questions of interest

  1. Which states see the highest volumes of fire-safety citations?
  2. What are the most common safety issues flagged during inspections?
  3. Can we predict whether a citation will be high severity?

Question 1: Which states see the highest volumes of fire-safety citations?

Hypothesis

States with larger nursing-home footprints may show the highest citation volume.

Answer

The notebook ranking places California and Ohio at the top of the state-citation chart.

Question 3: Can we predict whether a citation will be high severity?

Hypothesis

A model using state, deficiency category, inspection cycle, and resolution days may support screening, but class imbalance will limit certainty.

Answer

The reported Random Forest result has high overall accuracy but moderate recall for the rare high-severity class. It is better treated as a triage experiment than a dependable decision system.

Conclusion

Hypothesis partially supported. The reported 45% high-severity recall suggests possible screening value, but it also means more than half of high-severity cases may be missed at the evaluated operating point. Accuracy alone is insufficient for this rare-event problem.

Important caveat: These outputs represent statistical associations, not proof that any feature causes a severe outcome. Before operational use, check for data leakage (especially if resolution days are only known after the outcome), evaluate precision-recall and confusion matrices, calibrate probabilities, and validate on a later time period.

Takeaway

Data-informed oversight may help safety teams prioritize follow-up before deficiencies persist or worsen. Combining common deficiency patterns with carefully validated risk screening could support earlier intervention, but the analysis should complement—not replace—official inspection and professional judgment.
